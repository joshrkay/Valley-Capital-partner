A hotel collects occupancy and sales taxes from its guests and passes them to the government. That money is not the hotel's own. It is held for others, and tax authorities treat late or missing payments seriously.

A hotel under financial stress can fall behind. When it does, the balance matters to anyone who will deal with the hotel later.

## Why the balance matters

- **Penalties and interest** can add to the original amount.
- **Liens.** Unpaid taxes can become a lien on the property or the business, and property taxes in particular often rank ahead of a mortgage.
- **Responsibility can reach further than the business.** Some jurisdictions hold individuals who control the business responsible for taxes it collected and did not pay.
- **Successor liability.** In some places, a buyer of a business can become responsible for the seller's unpaid taxes unless the buyer takes certain steps, often by obtaining a clearance from the tax authority.

The rules differ by tax and by state or locality, so none of this can be assumed. It is a list of things to check.

## What to ask for

1. Filings and payment history for each tax and each jurisdiction, covering at least the last few years.
2. Any notices, assessments, audits, or payment plans.
3. Confirmation from the authority, where one is available, that the account is current.
4. Whether any collected taxes are being held, or have been spent.

## How it affects price and structure

A known balance can be deducted from the price, paid at closing, or held back until the authority confirms the position. In a [receivership](/glossary/#receivership), keeping taxes current is part of the receiver's job, and balances are usually settled at closing under the sale order.

Taxes are one of several liabilities to investigate, alongside payroll, vendor balances, and advance deposits. The [diligence checklist](/library/checklist/) lists the documents to request.
