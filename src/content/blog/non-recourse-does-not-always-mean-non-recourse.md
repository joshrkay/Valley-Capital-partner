Owners often describe a hotel loan as non-recourse. They mean that if the loan is not repaid, the lender can take the hotel and nothing else. That is the starting position in most commercial loans. It is not always where a loan ends up: the protection depends on conduct, and the wording of the [guaranty](/glossary/#guaranty) decides what one lapse costs.

## What non-recourse usually means

A non-recourse loan limits the lender to its collateral, which is the hotel and the assets that go with it. If the hotel is worth less than the debt, the lender absorbs the difference. The owners are not personally liable for the debt itself.

## Where carve-outs come in

Most non-recourse loans come with a separate promise from the owner's principals, called a [non-recourse carve-out](/glossary/#non-recourse-carve-out) guaranty, and sometimes a "bad boy" guaranty. It lists the acts that remove the protection. The list differs from loan to loan. Common entries are:

- Fraud or misrepresentation to the lender.
- Taking rents, insurance proceeds, or other money that belonged to the property.
- Transferring the hotel, or ownership of the borrower, without the lender's consent.
- Letting taxes, liens, or insurance go unpaid.
- A voluntary bankruptcy filing, or one the owners help bring about.

In some loans the list also reaches the franchise, so losing the flag can be one of the triggers. Each entry is an act that cuts what the lender can recover from the hotel or hinders enforcement. The guarantor is a separate person or entity that signed the guaranty, and owning the hotel through a company does not shield a guarantor from that promise.

## Two kinds of exposure

The wording decides how large the exposure is. A loss-based carve-out makes the guarantor liable only for the losses the lender suffers because of the act, which the lender must show. A full-debt carve-out makes the guarantor liable for the whole debt once the act occurs, whatever the loss.

The same act can fall in either kind in different guaranties. Defined terms matter as much: what counts as a transfer, or as helping to bring about a bankruptcy, can be wider than an owner expects.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A loan has a balance of $10 million, and a sale of the hotel would net $7 million. In a default, the owners move $200,000 of hotel receipts to an affiliate, which the guaranty lists as a carve-out. A loss-based guaranty makes the guarantor owe the $200,000 lost. A full-debt guaranty makes the guarantor liable for the $3 million left after the sale, before interest and costs. The act is the same, and the exposure is fifteen times larger.

## Why hotels raise the stakes

A hotel handles cash every day, collects occupancy and sales taxes for the government, depends on a franchise it can lose, and usually has a manager who controls the bank accounts. That gives it more ways to trip a carve-out than a building with a handful of leases, some outside the owner's sight. Whether a manager's error reaches the guarantor depends on how the guaranty defines the act and whose conduct counts.

## Steps owners take in a default

Three steps are common under pressure, and a guaranty may reach each of them.

**Moving cash.** Paying an affiliate, or another debt of the owner, from hotel receipts can look like taking money that belonged to the property. Paying the hotel's own payroll, taxes, and insurance is different. The loan documents draw the line.

**Transferring interests.** Admitting a new investor can count as a transfer of ownership, even when the aim is to raise cash to cure the default. The usual route is to ask for the lender's consent in writing first.

**Filing for bankruptcy.** A filing can pause a foreclosure, which is why it tempts. Some guaranties treat a voluntary filing as a full-debt trigger.

## Releases in a negotiated ending

In a [discounted payoff](/glossary/#discounted-payoff) or a [deed in lieu](/glossary/#deed-in-lieu), the owner usually asks for a release of the guaranty. The lender weighs what it gives up, a claim against the guarantor that may help both recovery and negotiation, against what it gains: the payment or the hotel without a contest, and saved time and cost. The guarantor's finances, and any carve-out already triggered, shift the balance.

A release is only as good as the signed agreement behind it, and it may be partial. A [note sale](/glossary/#note-sale) does not rewrite the guaranty. It passes to the new holder with the rest of the loan documents, as [what changes, and what does not, when your hotel loan is sold](/blog/when-your-hotel-loan-is-sold/) explains. What can change is the holder's willingness to agree to a release or a limit.

## What to check

- Read the guaranty itself, not the summary of terms.
- For each listed act, find out whether it makes the guarantor liable for losses or for the full debt.
- Read the definitions behind the triggers, such as transfer and bankruptcy.
- Note which triggers depend on a choice the borrower makes, and which can happen without one, such as a manager's error.
- Before a transfer, a filing, or moving cash between accounts, speak to the guarantor's own counsel, and ask the lender for consent in writing where the guaranty allows it.

## What we don't know

These documents vary by lender, by securitization, and by state. Whether a particular act triggers liability is a legal question about particular words. A post cannot say how a court would read an unclear clause, or whether a given holder would pursue a claim it has. Nothing here replaces counsel who has read the guaranty.
