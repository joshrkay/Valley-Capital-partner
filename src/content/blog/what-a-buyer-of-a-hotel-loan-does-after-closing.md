A defaulted hotel loan can change hands in a day. What happens next takes longer, and it follows a fairly regular order. This describes common practice, not any one buyer's. Each step asks for something the owner can prepare, and the ending depends on what the hotel and the owner can deliver.

## The first weeks

**Notice and payment instructions.** The owner is told who the new holder is and where payments go. The [assignment of the mortgage](/glossary/#assignment-of-mortgage) is usually recorded in the land records. The buyer needs payments to reach the right account from the start, and the owner can check the instructions with a contact already known before sending money.

**A review of the file.** The buyer had the loan file before it bid. Now it goes back through the servicing history, earlier agreements with the owner, the guarantor's status, the franchise, and taxes and insurance, looking for differences between the file and the hotel. The owner helps by keeping signed copies of every agreement and a record of each payment.

**A request for information.** Operating statements, performance reports, the franchise status, and the [improvement plan](/glossary/#pip) and its deadline. A buyer cannot judge a hotel from a stale statement, so current, dated figures that match the manager's reports speed everything after.

**A conversation.** What the owner wants, what the hotel can carry, and what capital the owner can raise. Some holders ask first for a [prenegotiation letter](/glossary/#prenegotiation-letter), which lets both sides talk without giving up their rights. The owner's counsel should read it, and the owner should arrive knowing the goal.

## The five usual endings

- **A [modification](/glossary/#loan-modification) or extension,** sometimes after a [forbearance](/glossary/#forbearance) that buys time, fits when the hotel can carry its debt on reset terms and the owner is cooperative. The holder usually wants current numbers and conditions such as a fee or a paydown. It breaks when the numbers do not support the new terms, or the improvement plan cannot be funded.
- **A [discounted payoff](/glossary/#discounted-payoff)** fits when the owner can raise new capital or refinance part of the balance. The owner has to show the money and a closing date, and the holder has to agree the figure and release its lien. It breaks when funds arrive late, or other creditors, such as tax authorities, have claims too.
- **A negotiated transfer of the hotel,** often by [deed in lieu of foreclosure](/glossary/#deed-in-lieu), fits when the owner prefers to exit and wants a release, sometimes of the guaranty. The owner has to hand over a hotel still operating, with franchise, license, and staff in place. It breaks when the brand will not accept the new owner, a license cannot move in time, or unpaid taxes complicate title.
- **A sale of the hotel by the owner,** with the loan repaid from the proceeds, fits when a purchaser will pay a price the holder accepts as payment of the loan. The holder has to agree the payoff and the timetable. It breaks when the purchaser's franchise application, financing, or license transfer fails.
- **Enforcement.** [Foreclosure](/glossary/#foreclosure), usually with a [receiver](/glossary/#receivership) running the hotel meanwhile, fits when no agreement can be reached. The holder has to follow the documents and the court's process, and fund costs and advances. A bankruptcy filing can pause it, and a court's calendar can slow it.

Many buyers prefer an agreed ending, because enforcement is slower and costs more. Where no agreement can be reached they rely on the rights in the loan documents.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A holder has a $10 million loan on a hotel now worth $8 million. A discounted payoff funded by outside capital brings in $7 million in month 3. A deed in lieu in month 4 hands over the hotel, and the holder spends $0.5 million on improvements and sale costs before selling in month 10 for $7.8 million, netting $7.3 million. Enforcement runs 18 months, the hotel slips to $7 million, and costs of $1 million leave $6 million.

Enforcement looks strongest on paper, because it ends in title to a hotel worth $8 million today. Here it is the slowest and nets the least. Change the facts, such as a fast foreclosure state or a secure flag, and the order can change. The comparison to make is net figures and dates, not headline values.

## What drives speed

Cash flow decides most. If the hotel's income covers its debt payments, a modification is possible. If not, the choice narrows to a payoff, a transfer, or enforcement. The brand keeps its own clock through cure periods and improvement deadlines, and the owner's capital, the guarantor's position, and how long [enforcement takes in that state](/blog/foreclosure-timelines-and-hotel-value/) all weigh in.

There is no fixed timeline, so the owner cannot plan around a date the buyer names. An agreed ending can take months. Enforcement can take longer, especially where foreclosure runs through the courts. The dates that bind are in the documents: the maturity date, the end of a forbearance, and the brand's deadlines.

## Buyers differ

Buyers differ in what they want the end result to be. Some prefer the loan to keep performing, so their interest runs with the hotel's recovery. Others expect to end up with the hotel, and for them a modification is one option weighed against enforcement. The two can react differently to a request for more time or a release. An owner is entitled to ask a buyer which it is, and to ask for the answer in writing. A written answer is not a promise, because facts change, but it sets a baseline for reading later conduct. For the owner's side, see [what changes, and what does not, when your hotel loan is sold](/blog/when-your-hotel-loan-is-sold/).

## What we don't know

We have not seen a fixed order or timetable that every buyer follows, and we do not describe one here. Sequence and timing depend on the loan documents, the hotel, and the state. Which ending a particular buyer prefers cannot be known from outside.
