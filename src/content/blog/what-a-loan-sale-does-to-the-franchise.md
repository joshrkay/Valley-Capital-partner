"Will I lose my [flag](/glossary/#flag)?" is one of the first questions a hotel owner asks when a lender sells the loan. The short answer is that the sale does not, by itself, change the franchise. Several things around a sale can, and most of them run on a clock the brand sets.

## What stays the same

The [franchise agreement](/glossary/#franchise-agreement) is between the brand and the owner. The lender is not a party to it, as [the franchise when a hotel loan is sold](/library/franchise/) explains. A [note sale](/glossary/#note-sale) changes who holds the debt, not who holds the franchise. The fees, the standards, the inspections, and the [improvement plan](/glossary/#pip) carry on under the same terms.

## Why a brand cares who controls the hotel

A brand earns fees from the hotel and puts its name on the door. A guest cannot tell who owns the building, so the brand answers for the stay. That is why it sets standards and inspects, and why it cares who stands behind a hotel: whether fees will be paid and the required work funded. The franchise was granted to a particular owner, so a change in control makes the brand ask what it is now dealing with.

## What draws the brand's attention

- **Problems that already exist.** Unpaid fees, an overdue improvement plan, or a failed quality inspection can already give the brand grounds to act. A buyer's request that the brand confirm them puts attention on them.
- **A transfer of the hotel.** If the loan ends in a foreclosure, a deed in lieu, or a negotiated transfer, the hotel changes hands. A franchise agreement usually cannot pass to a new owner without the brand's consent, and a new owner usually applies for its own franchise.
- **The loan documents.** Some loans treat a franchise default as a default on the loan. Some list losing the flag among the triggers in a [non-recourse carve-out](/glossary/#non-recourse-carve-out) guaranty.

## The comfort letter, step by step

The [comfort letter](/glossary/#comfort-letter) is signed by the brand, the owner, and the lender, and it has three working parts. **Notice:** the brand tells the lender when the owner is in default under the franchise agreement. **Cure:** the lender gets time to fix the default, for example by paying unpaid fees or funding overdue work, before the brand acts. **A new franchise:** if the lender takes title, the letter gives a path to a franchise for the new owner.

Whether these rights pass to a buyer of the loan depends on the letter. Many need the brand's consent, or a replacement letter. If the rights do not pass, the new holder may learn of a franchise default late. The brand's consent usually also governs a transfer of the hotel, a new owner's own franchise, and, in a receivership, a receiver operating under the flag, often for a short term.

## Why time is the scarce thing

A brand's notice starts a cure period. Its length is set by the franchise agreement and the comfort letter, and it is not known from outside. A buyer's review, the seller's consent to approach the brand, the brand's reply, and any improvement work all draw on that period, so each party's useful moves come early.

**An owner** can bring fees current, ask the brand to agree the improvement schedule in writing, and read the franchise agreement's transfer terms before any talk of a transfer.

**A lender** selling the loan can read the comfort letter's assignment terms first, and decide early whether to ask the brand for consent or a replacement letter.

**A buyer** can read the letter before bidding, ask the brand to confirm standing, fees, and improvements with the seller's consent, and allow time for a new franchise application if the hotel may change hands.

## What losing the flag costs

A hotel that loses its flag loses much of its demand, because the brand supplies the name, the reservation system, and the loyalty program. Many franchise agreements also charge liquidated damages if they end early.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A hotel has a $9 million loan with debt payments of $900,000 a year. Under the flag it earns $1.2 million of net operating income, a [debt service coverage ratio](/glossary/#dscr) of 1.33. The brand terminates after an overdue improvement plan and an unused cure period. As an independent hotel it earns $700,000, a ratio of 0.78 and a $200,000 yearly shortfall, and the brand claims $400,000 in liquidated damages. A buyer of the loan now prices the hotel on the lower income.

## What we don't know

Whether a sale of the loan alone can trigger anything under a particular franchise agreement or comfort letter. We found no published source that addresses a sale of the loan, as opposed to a foreclosure, and brands differ from one another. The answer is in the documents, and sometimes in what the brand says when it is asked.
