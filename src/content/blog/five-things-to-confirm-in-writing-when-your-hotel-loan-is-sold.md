When a lender sells a hotel loan, the owner usually learns of it from a letter. The letter is the start of the conversation, not the end of it. Five points are worth having in writing before anything changes, because the loan has a new party on the other end, and an answer given by phone is hard to rely on later.

## 1. Who holds the loan now

Payments, notices, and proposals need to reach the party that can act on them. A good answer gives the holder's legal name, its address for notices, and the details of the [assignment of the mortgage](/glossary/#assignment-of-mortgage), which is usually recorded in the local land records.

Recording can lag closing, so a missing record on the first day proves little. A refusal to name the holder, or a name that matches nothing on record, calls for counsel.

## 2. Who services it

The holder and the servicer can be different firms. The servicer collects payments, and the holder decides. A good answer says who receives payments, by what method, and from which date.

Changed payment instructions need the most care. An email that sends money to a new account is a known way to misdirect funds. Confirm any change through a contact already known, such as the number on earlier loan statements, before sending anything. Urgency, and instructions that arrive only by email, are warning signs.

## 3. What is owed

"What is owed" can mean three figures: the unpaid principal, the amount to cure the default, or the payoff amount. Ask for a written statement, as of a stated date, that says which and itemizes it: principal, interest, default interest if the documents provide for it, fees, and any [protective advances](/glossary/#protective-advance). It should also state what accrues each day after that date.

Read it against the loan documents and the owner's payment records. Check that every payment appears, that default interest starts on a date the documents allow, and that each advance has an invoice. A single total with no lines gives nothing to check.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A payoff statement as of the 15th shows principal of $5,000,000, interest of $60,000, default interest of $30,000, fees of $10,000, and advances of $50,000, $15,000 of it for insurance. The total is $5,150,000, plus $1,000 for each day after the 15th. The owner paid that insurance, so the advance is queried, and the default interest start date is checked against the documents.

## 4. Which earlier agreements still stand

A [forbearance](/glossary/#forbearance), a [modification](/glossary/#loan-modification), an extension letter, or a payment plan. The loan documents pass to the new holder as they are, so these should carry over. But the new holder works from the file it was sent, and the file can be incomplete.

Ask the holder to list each agreement it has, with its date, to say that each remains in effect, and to state any end date or unmet condition. Compare the list with the owner's signed copies. A side understanding that was never written down is the one most at risk.

## 5. Who to talk to

A conversation with someone who cannot decide costs time that a hotel in default may not have. A good answer names a person, says whether that person can agree to terms, says who else must approve, and says how proposals should be sent. A shared mailbox and a repeated promise to pass things along are warning signs.

## If the answers do not come

Keep meeting the payment obligations in the loan documents, to the party that has been confirmed, and tell the owner's counsel. Send each request again in writing to the notice address the transfer letter gives, keep a copy, and note the date.

If no holder can be confirmed, counsel can check the land records and advise how to document payments meanwhile. If no payoff statement arrives, ask again and state the figure the owner's records show. If a forbearance's end date is unclear, send the owner's signed copy and ask the holder to confirm it. The earlier post on [what changes, and what does not, when your hotel loan is sold](/blog/when-your-hotel-loan-is-sold/) covers what else to expect.

## What we don't know

Whether and how a lender must notify a borrower of a sale depends on the loan documents and on state law. So does whether a holder must answer these questions on request, and how soon. Reading yours is the first step.
