A [discounted payoff](/glossary/#discounted-payoff), often called a DPO, is an agreement in which a lender accepts less than the full balance as payment in full. For a lender with a troubled hotel loan it has a clear appeal: the loan ends on a known date, at a known figure, without a foreclosure.

It is not always available. A discounted payoff needs someone with money, whether the owner, a new investor, or a refinancing lender, and it only makes sense if the figure compares well with the lender's other choices.

## What a lender compares it with

The alternatives are covered in [extend, sell, or foreclose](/library/choices/). The useful comparison is net of everything: the recovery from a longer process, less the legal costs, [protective advances](/glossary/#protective-advance), receiver costs, and the months it takes, set against a certain payment now.

## Points that decide whether it works

- **Source of funds.** Where is the money coming from, how sure is it, and what is the timetable? A deposit and proof of funds separate a real offer from a hope.
- **The borrower's wider position.** Other creditors, tax liens, and the status of the franchise can all affect whether the hotel is worth saving, and whether the payoff will actually close.
- **Guarantors.** The lender is usually asked to release any [guaranty](/glossary/#guaranty). What the lender gives up should be weighed against what it receives.
- **Documents.** A payoff letter, a release of the lien, and releases of claims all need to be agreed in advance.
- **Approvals and accounting.** A discount normally means a loss against the carrying value, so internal approvals, and sometimes regulatory classification, come into it.

## Where outside capital fits

Owners who want a discounted payoff often cannot fund it alone. Capital from a third party, secured by the hotel, is a common way to close the gap. That is worth knowing for lenders, because it means a discounted payoff offer may arrive from a source other than the borrower.

A lender that is considering an offer should ask for the same things it would ask of any purchaser: proof of funds, a closing date, and clarity about who is on the other side.
