A hotel earns its income one night at a time, so its operating statement reads differently from an office building's. A buyer of a hotel loan reads it for what it says about the business, then adjusts it. The figure on the page is the owner's version of income. The figure a buyer prices from is its own.

## Start with three numbers

Three measures sit at the top of almost every hotel report. [Occupancy](/glossary/#occupancy) is the share of rooms sold. [Average daily rate](/glossary/#adr) is what each sold room earned. [RevPAR](/glossary/#revpar) combines the two into revenue per available room.

Each is more useful next to the hotel's [competitive set](/glossary/#comp-set). Divide the hotel's RevPAR by the set's to get an index, and watch it over time. A hotel losing ground to its neighbors has a different problem from one in a soft market, and the two call for different answers.

## Look at the trend, not the snapshot

A [trailing twelve-month](/glossary/#t12) statement removes the effect of the season. Several years side by side show whether results are falling, recovering, or bouncing around. A hotel in trouble often shows a clear change in direction, and the date of that change is worth finding. It may line up with a new manager, a lost franchise, a closed floor, or a new competitor.

## Red flags, and why each matters

Each of these can make income look better than the business is.

- **Maintenance cut to save money.** Trimmed repairs and payroll raise income now. The catch-up cost lands on the next owner. Compare the repairs line with the building's age and the brand's last inspection.
- **An under-funded reserve.** If the [furniture, fixtures, and equipment reserve](/glossary/#ffe-reserve) was skipped or drawn down to cover shortfalls, income is overstated by what should have been set aside. Ask who controls the account.
- **Income that will not repeat.** Insurance proceeds, a settlement, or a one-time event can sit in revenue. It is not next year's income.
- **Related-party fees.** Fees paid to companies the owner controls may be waived in a bad year or set above market. Neither shows what a new manager would cost.
- **A falling index.** A rising market can lift the hotel's own numbers while its share shrinks.
- **A shifting customer mix.** Occupancy held up by discounts or a few large accounts is less secure than the same occupancy at steady rates.

## From reported income to income a buyer can rely on

Net operating income, less the reserve, is what is left to pay the loan. Dividing it by annual payments gives the [debt service coverage ratio](/glossary/#dscr).

Normalizing means rebuilding that income the way a new owner would experience it. A buyer adds back costs that will not recur, such as a one-time legal bill, if an invoice supports them. It deducts income that will not recur, any reserve that was not funded, and the gap between an affiliate's fee and what an outside manager would charge.

Two careful readers reach different numbers from the same page, because they disagree about which costs recur and how much reserve to hold. An add-back is a claim about the future, so a proposed one is a claim to test, not a fact.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A statement shows net operating income of $900,000 on a $7.5 million loan, a [debt yield](/glossary/#debt-yield) of 12%. The buyer deducts $50,000 of one-time insurance proceeds, $90,000 for a required reserve left unfunded, and $40,000 for an affiliate's below-market fee, then adds back a $30,000 legal bill. The result is $750,000 and 10%. A reader who rejects the add-back gets $720,000 and 9.6%.

## Questions to put to the owner or manager

1. Is the statement prepared on the standard [USALI](/glossary/#usali) basis, so it can be compared with other hotels?
2. Which adjustments or add-backs are proposed, who proposed them, and what documents support each?
3. What is in revenue that will not repeat, and which fees go to companies the owner controls?
4. Are any bills being deferred? A hotel under stress often falls behind on taxes, vendors, or payroll, which can make a statement look healthier than the business is. See [the taxes that can follow a hotel](/blog/taxes-that-can-follow-a-hotel/).
5. Do reported results match the manager's monthly performance reports and the brand's data?

A statement says what happened. A visit to the hotel, a conversation with the manager, and the brand's latest inspection explain why. How far to trust any of it depends on who prepared it and whether anyone outside the hotel has checked it.
