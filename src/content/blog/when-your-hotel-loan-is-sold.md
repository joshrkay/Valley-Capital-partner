Hotel owners are sometimes told that their lender has sold their loan. It can be unsettling, but the position is simpler than it feels. A [note sale](/glossary/#note-sale) changes who holds the loan. It does not change the loan.

Three misunderstandings are common. A sale does not reset a default, it does not void an agreement the owner already has, and it does not hand the buyer the hotel.

## What stays the same

The note, the mortgage, and any [guaranty](/glossary/#guaranty) stay as they are. The obligations and rights in those documents pass to the new holder unchanged, including the maturity date and the terms of any [forbearance](/glossary/#forbearance) or [modification](/glossary/#loan-modification) already in place.

A default stays a default. What the buyer paid is between the buyer and the seller. The owner still owes what the documents say, unless the new holder agrees to less. The owner also still owns the hotel. The buyer holds a lien and the right to enforce it, and to take the hotel it must follow the same process the original lender would have followed.

## What changes

The person making decisions. A holder's room to negotiate depends on what it paid for the loan, not on the balance, so a new holder may agree to terms the original lender could not. It may also read the hotel, the franchise, and the timetable differently. Neither is certain, which is why it is worth finding out early.

## Agreements already in place

A forbearance or modification passes with the loan, but its wording decides what it is worth. Read it for whether it binds successors and assigns, when it expires, and what the owner must keep doing to stay within it, such as payments, reports, or milestones.

Check what is in writing. A promise made in conversation or by email to the old lender's officer may not bind a new holder, and whether it does depends on the documents and the state. Ask the new holder to say in writing which agreements it will honor.

## The guarantor's position

A sale neither releases a guarantor nor adds liability. The guaranty passes on the same wording, and that wording decides whether it covers the whole debt or only certain acts. What can change is the new holder's willingness to limit or release it, which is often part of a negotiated transfer of the hotel.

## What to expect

- **A notice of transfer.** It should say who the new holder is and where payments are to go. Whether a lender must give notice, and what it must say, depends on the loan documents and state law.
- **A request for information.** Operating statements, performance reports, and the status of the franchise and any [improvement plan](/glossary/#pip) are typical.
- **A proposal.** That might be a modification, a payoff figure, or a negotiated transfer of the hotel.

There is no fixed timetable, and holders' aims differ. An owner can ask what outcome the holder expects.

## What an owner can do

1. Read the notice carefully, and confirm the new payment instructions in writing before changing anything. Do not keep paying the old lender, and do not stop paying.
2. Gather current operating statements, the franchise status, and a clear picture of any renovation requirements.
3. Be ready to explain what you want: to keep the hotel, to pay off at a discount, or to exit.
4. Date and keep every notice, payment, and proposal, and put requests in writing.
5. Speak to your own counsel before negotiating, especially about any guaranty and before moving cash or transferring ownership. If the franchise is at risk, speak to the brand too: the franchise agreement is between the brand and the owner, not the lender.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** An owner owes $6 million on a hotel worth $5 million, with $500,000 of brand improvements due. The holder would accept $4 million as payoff. Paying off takes $4.5 million of new money and leaves $500,000 of equity. Holding leaves the owner $1 million underwater, before the improvements. A transfer ends the debt, and the guaranty if the release says so, but leaves no equity.

Each path also depends on something outside the arithmetic: the money, the holder's agreement, or a signed release. Most owners and holders prefer an agreed outcome, because it is faster and cheaper for both. Where agreement cannot be reached, the holder can rely on the rights in the loan documents, which is why the same preparation matters either way.
