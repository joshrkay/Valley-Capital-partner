Some hotels sit on leased land. The owner owns the building but pays rent to a landowner under a [ground lease](/glossary/#ground-lease). In a workout, that lease is part of the collateral, and it can decide what a foreclosure would actually deliver.

A lender with a mortgage on a ground-leased hotel holds a leasehold interest, not ownership of the land. If the ground lease ends, so does the collateral, so the term left and the ways it could end early come first.

## Why the remaining term matters

Value falls as the term runs down, because a buyer is paying for a building on land it must eventually give back. Compare the remaining term with the loan's maturity, the franchise term, and the time a workout is likely to take.

A buyer's own lender will usually want the lease to run well past its loan, so a short term can shrink the pool of buyers as well as the price. Renewal rights matter too: who may use them, on what conditions, and by what deadline.

## What to check in the documents

- **Rent.** The current amount, scheduled increases, any reset to a new level on a set date, and arrears.
- **Lender protections.** Leasehold mortgage provisions often give the lender notice of a default under the ground lease, a right to cure it, and sometimes a right to a new lease if the old one is terminated.
- **Consents.** The landowner may have to approve a transfer of the leasehold, including one that results from a foreclosure or a [deed in lieu](/glossary/#deed-in-lieu).
- **Other rights.** Purchase options, rights of first refusal, and limits on use or on the type of hotel.
- **The landowner's lender.** If the land is mortgaged and the lease ranks below that mortgage, a foreclosure on the land could threaten the lease, unless the landowner's lender has agreed that the lease survives.

An [estoppel certificate](/glossary/#estoppel) from the landowner confirms that the lease is in effect, what its terms are, and whether any defaults exist. It is one of the most useful documents in the file.

## When the ground lease goes into default

If the owner stops paying ground rent or breaks another term, the landowner may be entitled to end the lease, and the collateral ends with it. Protecting it can mean paying the rent, which the lender may add to the loan as a [protective advance](/glossary/#protective-advance).

A cure right is only as good as its details: the notice period, the address notices go to, and whether the default is one a lender can cure at all. A borrower's insolvency, for example, may not be.

## When the loan is sold

Lender protections belong to the lender named in the lease, or one that fits its definition. A lease may require notice to the landowner of an assignment of the loan, the landowner's consent, or that the holder meet a test such as being an institutional lender. If the buyer does not meet it, the protections may not pass.

A buyer should ask the landowner to confirm in writing that it recognizes the new holder and where notices go.

## How it shows up in value

Ground rent reduces the cash flow available for the loan, and an increase or reset reduces it further, so a model should carry the future rent, not today's. An appraisal should state which interest it values: a hotel valued as if the owner held the land overstates the collateral. A buyer of the hotel will also need financing, and its lender will look at the same term, rent, and protections.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A hotel earns $1.5 million a year before ground rent and owes $1 million a year in debt service. Ground rent is $200,000, so cash flow after rent is $1.3 million and [debt service coverage](/glossary/#dscr) is 1.3. The lease resets rent to $500,000 on a set date. Cash flow falls to $1 million and coverage to 1.0.

A 10% dip in income before rent, or $150,000, then takes coverage to 0.85. The loan did not change. The lease did. If the reset date falls inside the workout, it belongs in the first review.

The [diligence checklist](/library/checklist/) includes the ground lease among the property documents to collect. Which protections the lease gives, and whether they survive a loan sale, depend on the lease and on state law.
