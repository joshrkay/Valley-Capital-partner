A hotel's financial statements look different from those of an office building or an apartment block, because a hotel earns its income one night at a time. Before anyone prices a loan on a hotel, the operating statement has to be read for what it says about the business, not only the building.

## Start with three numbers

Three measures sit at the top of almost every hotel report. [Occupancy](/glossary/#occupancy) is the share of rooms sold. [Average daily rate](/glossary/#adr) is what each sold room earned. [RevPAR](/glossary/#revpar) combines the two into revenue per available room.

Each is more useful next to the hotel's [competitive set](/glossary/#comp-set). A hotel that is losing ground to the hotels around it has a different problem from one whose whole market is soft, and the two call for different answers.

## Look at the trend, not the snapshot

A [trailing twelve-month](/glossary/#t12) statement removes the effect of the season. Looking at several years shows whether results are falling, recovering, or simply bouncing around. A hotel in trouble often shows a clear change in direction, and the date of that change is worth finding. It may line up with a new manager, a lost franchise, a closed floor, or a new competitor.

## Then read down the page

- **Revenue mix.** How much comes from rooms, and how much from food and beverage, meeting space, or other sources.
- **Fixed and variable costs.** Payroll, utilities, insurance, and property taxes behave differently from fees that move with revenue.
- **Brand and management fees.** Franchise royalties, marketing and reservation charges, and management fees are paid before the owner sees a dollar.
- **The reserve.** A hotel is expected to set aside money for [furniture, fixtures, and equipment](/glossary/#ffe-reserve). Check that it has, and who controls the account.

Net operating income, less that reserve, is what is left to pay the loan. Dividing it by annual payments gives the [debt service coverage ratio](/glossary/#dscr).

## Questions worth asking about the numbers

1. Is the statement prepared on the standard [USALI](/glossary/#usali) basis, so it can be compared with other hotels?
2. Are there adjustments or add-backs, and who proposed them?
3. Are any bills being deferred? A hotel under stress often falls behind on taxes, vendors, or payroll, which can make a statement look healthier than the business is.
4. Do reported results match the manager's monthly performance reports and the brand's data?

A statement says what happened. A visit to the hotel, a conversation with the manager, and the brand's latest inspection explain why.
