Many hotels serve alcohol, whether in a restaurant, a bar, a banquet hall, or through room service. The license that allows it is usually issued to a particular business for particular premises, and it is regulated by the state or locality. Rules differ widely, so the only safe general advice is to find out early.

The license does not simply move with the property or the loan. When ownership changes, or a [receiver](/glossary/#receivership) takes over, the new party usually needs its own approval or an interim arrangement, and the time that takes is the risk.

## Why it matters to revenue

If a hotel loses its ability to serve alcohol, the loss is more than a bar. Restaurant revenue, banquets, and events can all be affected, and groups that book on the basis of what the hotel can serve may book elsewhere. In a hotel already under pressure, that can be the difference between operating and not.

## Why it does not move on its own

A foreclosure, a sale, a deed in lieu, and a receiver's appointment change who controls the premises. They do not change who holds the license. A note sale changes only who holds the loan, so on its own it leaves the license where it is, though some states regulate who may hold a financial interest in a licensed business.

Whether a license can be pledged or sold at all, or can only be surrendered and reissued, differs by state.

## How a transfer or new application usually runs

The stages vary, but the shape is common:

1. The new applicant files, often with disclosures about its owners and managers.
2. The authority reviews the people behind it.
3. Notice periods, local approvals, or hearings may follow, and can add weeks or months.
4. The authority decides, and the license issues to the new holder.

Timing is the risk because a buyer can sometimes apply only once it has a contract or control of the premises, so the gap can fall just after closing. Where a state allows it, a temporary permit, or an interim operating agreement under which the current holder keeps serving while the application is pending, can bridge the gap. Some states offer neither.

## What to find in the file

A lender or buyer should establish who holds the license, whether the owner, the manager, or a separate restaurant operator, and what it covers. Then its status and renewal dates, any open violations, and any lien, claim, or agreement that touches it. An unresolved violation can follow the premises or complicate an application. The [diligence checklist](/library/checklist/) lists the license and permits among the operating documents.

A receiver works within the same limits. It does not automatically hold the license. It looks to the current holder, a temporary permit, or an interim arrangement where the state provides one. If none exists, it may have to stop beverage service while the rooms stay open.

## What a gap does to closing

If closing waits for the license, the hotel changes hands later and carries its costs for longer. If closing goes ahead, the buyer or receiver runs without beverage service until the license issues. Events already booked with deposits are the first exposure: each must be served, moved, or refunded.

The license can be a condition of closing, with a timeline that allows for the authority's process, or a planned gap with its cost priced in. Decide which before the date is set.

## Questions for counsel

- Who may hold the license after a sale or a receiver's appointment, and may the buyer apply before closing?
- Is a temporary permit or an interim operating arrangement available, and for how long?
- Does the authority need notice of the receiver's appointment, or of a change in the lender?
- Do any open violations affect an application?

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A hotel takes in $100,000 a month from its restaurant, bar, and banquets. About $40,000 of that depends on serving alcohol, and the events that book on that basis bring $30,000 a month in room revenue. If a transfer leaves a four-month gap with no interim arrangement, up to $280,000 of revenue is exposed.

Revenue is not profit, so the hotel's loss is the margin on it, but payroll for retained staff and other fixed costs keep running. A temporary arrangement covering two of the four months would halve the exposure to $140,000.
