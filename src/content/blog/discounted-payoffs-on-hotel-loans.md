A [discounted payoff](/glossary/#discounted-payoff), often called a DPO, is an agreement in which a lender accepts less than the full balance as payment in full. For a lender with a troubled hotel loan it has a clear appeal: the loan ends on a known date, at a known figure, without a foreclosure.

It is not always available, and not always the better choice. It needs someone with money to close it. Two questions decide it: is the price better than the alternatives after costs and delay, and will the money arrive?

## How a lender compares an offer

The alternatives are covered in [extend, sell, or foreclose](/library/choices/). The useful comparison is net of everything, and it runs in order.

1. Estimate what the longer path would realistically produce, such as a sale of the hotel after foreclosure.
2. Subtract the cost of getting there: legal fees, [protective advances](/glossary/#protective-advance), and receiver costs.
3. Allow for time. Money in many months is worth less than money now, and the hotel can lose value on the way.
4. Set the result against the offer, and against an indicative price for the note if the lender has asked for one.
5. Check the offer against the loan's [carrying value](/glossary/#carrying-value). A discount is normally a loss on the books, so internal approvals, and sometimes regulatory classification, come into it.

## What makes an offer credible

Owners who want a discounted payoff often cannot fund it alone, and capital from a third party, secured by the hotel, is a common way to close the gap. An offer can therefore come from someone other than the borrower. The lender should ask what it would ask of any purchaser.

Start with the source of funds. The owner's cash, a new investor, and a refinancing lender are different things, and [proof of funds](/glossary/#proof-of-funds) shows that money exists, not that it is committed to this deal. Money put down, and the terms on which it is forfeited, show how far the other side stands behind its timetable.

Third-party capital often brings its own conditions, such as a review period or an approval. Each is a way for the deal to fail while the lender waits.

## The terms behind the price

A payoff is not a [note sale](/glossary/#note-sale). In a payoff the loan ends and the lien is released. In a note sale the loan continues with a new holder. When a third party supplies the money, a deal can be built either way, and the structure changes what happens to the guaranty and the lien.

Four terms decide whether the lender is protected:

- **Releases.** Whether the lender releases only the borrower or also each [guaranty](/glossary/#guaranty), and whether the borrower releases claims against the lender in return.
- **Lien release and payoff letter.** The release should take effect only when funds arrive. The payoff letter states the amount due, the date through which it holds, and what changes if funds come later.
- **If funding slips.** How long the lender waits, whether the discount lapses, whether the deposit is kept, and whether enforcement continues meanwhile.
- **Confidentiality.** A discount given to one borrower can be cited by another.

## Why payoffs fail, and when a no is right

Most failures are slipped funding, a source that was not what it seemed, an unmet condition, or a change in the hotel while the deal waits, such as a franchise default, an unpaid tax, or another creditor's lien. The answers are a firm date, a deposit at signing, every condition asked for at the start, and a recheck of the franchise, tax, and title position before closing.

A no is a valid result when the figure is below the net of the longer path, the source cannot be shown, or the releases asked for are wider than the price justifies. A reasoned no, given early, costs the hotel less time than a yes that later falls through.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A lender holds a $10 million loan. An offer of $6 million would close in sixty days. The alternative is foreclosure with a receiver and a sale in about eighteen months, with $1 million of legal, receiver, and advance costs. A $7.5 million sale nets $6.5 million. A sale at $6.5 million nets $5.5 million.

The break-even sale price is $7 million. The choice turns on how well the hotel holds its value for eighteen months, and on whether the $6 million arrives.

The releases, lien mechanics, and remedies if funding slips all depend on the documents and state law.
