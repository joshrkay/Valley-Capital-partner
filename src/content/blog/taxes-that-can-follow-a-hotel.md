A hotel collects occupancy and sales taxes from its guests and passes them to the government. That money is not the hotel's own. It is held for others, and tax authorities treat late or missing payments seriously.

A hotel under financial stress can fall behind, and the balance matters to anyone who deals with the hotel later. Whether it follows the property, the next operator, or neither depends on the kind of obligation and on the state or locality.

## Four kinds of balance

- **Taxes collected for the government.** These are the occupancy and sales taxes above. Some jurisdictions hold the individuals who control the business responsible for them. Some let the authority pursue a buyer of the business unless the buyer takes certain steps, often by obtaining a clearance.
- **Payroll-related amounts.** Amounts withheld from employees' pay are also held for the government, and are usually treated much like collected taxes. Unpaid wages are the employer's debt, but staff who go unpaid leave, and the next operator inherits the gap.
- **Property taxes.** These attach to the real estate and often rank ahead of a mortgage. Of the four kinds, they are the most likely to follow the hotel itself to whoever holds it next.
- **Vendor and other balances.** Suppliers, utilities, and prepaid deposits are generally debts of the borrowing company, not of the building. They still reach a new operator in practice: vendors stop deliveries, and guests with deposits arrive expecting rooms.

Penalties and interest add to each, and unpaid taxes can become a lien on the property or the business. The rules differ by tax and by state or locality, so none of this can be assumed.

## How a buyer finds them

Start by reconciling. Tax owed on reported revenue should roughly match what the bank records show was paid. Set the income statement beside the payment history and the tax payable line on the balance sheet. Then ask for:

1. Filings and payment history for each tax and each jurisdiction, covering at least the last few years.
2. Any notices, assessments, audits, or payment plans.
3. Confirmation from the authority, where one is available, that the account is current.
4. The owner's or seller's own schedule of liabilities, checked against the rest.
5. Whether any collected taxes are being held, or have been spent.

A clean answer is dull: returns filed on time, payments that match what revenue implies, no notices, and a letter from the authority showing the account current. Warning signs are a tax payable balance that grows month after month, a payment plan, late or missing returns, and a habit of paying vendors and payroll first and taxes later.

## How exposure is sized and priced

The exposure is the tax owed, plus penalties and interest through the expected closing date, plus any returns not yet filed. Interest keeps running while a process drags on, so delay makes the number larger. A buyer allows for the chance that an audit finds more.

Who carries it depends on what is sold. A buyer of a note takes the loan, not the hotel or its tax history, and loan sales are often made as-is. The balance stays with the borrower, but it reduces what the collateral can repay, so the buyer prices it.

A holder may also pay property taxes to protect its lien. That [protective advance](/glossary/#protective-advance) is added to the loan balance, which makes the loan larger against the same collateral.

A buyer of the hotel is the one an authority may look to, as may a note buyer that later takes the hotel and runs it. The buyer can ask for the balance to be deducted from the price, paid at closing, or held back until the authority confirms the position.

In a [receivership](/glossary/#receivership), keeping taxes current is part of the receiver's job, and balances are usually settled at closing under the sale order.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A buyer is pricing a note at $4.5 million. The hotel collected $15,000 a month in occupancy tax and remitted none for 12 months, so $180,000 is owed, and the buyer estimates $30,000 more in penalties and interest by closing. Unpaid property taxes add $60,000. The total is $270,000, or 6% of the price, and the buyer lowers its bid by that amount.

The [diligence checklist](/library/checklist/) lists the documents to request. Counsel who knows the state should confirm which balances follow a buyer before closing.
