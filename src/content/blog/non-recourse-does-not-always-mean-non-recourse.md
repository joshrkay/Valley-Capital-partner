Owners often describe a hotel loan as non-recourse. They mean that if the loan is not repaid, the lender can take the hotel and nothing else. That is the starting position in most commercial loans. It is not always where a loan ends up, because the protection depends on conduct, and the wording of the [guaranty](/glossary/#guaranty) decides how much a single lapse costs.

## What non-recourse usually means

A non-recourse loan limits the lender to its collateral, which is the hotel and the assets that go with it. If the hotel is worth less than the debt, the lender absorbs the difference. The owners are not personally liable for the debt itself.

## Where carve-outs come in

Most non-recourse loans come with a separate promise from the owner's principals, called a [non-recourse carve-out](/glossary/#non-recourse-carve-out) guaranty, and sometimes a "bad boy" guaranty. It lists the acts that remove the protection. The list differs from loan to loan. Common entries are:

- Fraud or misrepresentation to the lender.
- Taking rents, insurance proceeds, or other money that belonged to the property.
- Transferring the hotel, or ownership of the borrower, without the lender's consent.
- Letting taxes, liens, or insurance go unpaid.
- A voluntary bankruptcy filing, or one the owners help bring about.

In some loans the list also reaches the franchise, so losing the flag can be one of the triggers.

The entries share a purpose. Each is an act that reduces what the lender can recover from the hotel, or gets in the way of enforcing against it. The borrower is usually a company that owns the hotel. The guarantor is a separate person or entity that signed the guaranty, and owning the hotel through a company does not shield a guarantor from a promise the guarantor made.

## Two kinds of exposure

The wording decides how large the exposure is. A loss-based carve-out makes the guarantor liable only for the losses the lender suffers because of the act. The lender has to show the loss and connect it to the act. A full-debt carve-out makes the guarantor liable for the whole debt once the act occurs, whatever the size of the loss.

The same act can fall in either kind. A lapsed insurance policy may be a loss-based trigger in one guaranty and a full-debt trigger in another. Defined terms matter as much. What counts as a transfer, a bankruptcy, or help in bringing one about can be wider or narrower than an owner expects.

**Illustration (invented numbers):** A hotel loan has a balance of $10 million, and a sale of the hotel would net $7 million. In a default, the owners move $200,000 of hotel receipts to an affiliate, an act the guaranty lists as a carve-out. If the guaranty is loss-based, the guarantor owes the $200,000 the lender lost. If it is full-debt, the guarantor is liable for the whole $10 million, less what the lender recovers from the hotel. After the sale, that is $3 million, before interest and costs. The act is the same. The exposure is fifteen times larger, and the difference is one clause.

## Why hotels raise the stakes

A hotel handles cash every day, collects occupancy and sales taxes for the government, depends on a franchise it can lose, and usually has a manager who controls the bank accounts. That gives it more ways to trip a carve-out than a building with a handful of leases. The owner may not see every transaction. Whether a manager's error or a lapsed franchise reaches the guarantor depends on how the guaranty defines the act and whose conduct counts.

## Steps owners take in a default

Three steps are common under pressure, often for good reasons, and a guaranty may reach each of them.

**Moving cash.** Paying an affiliate, or another debt of the owner, from hotel receipts can look like taking money that belonged to the property. Paying the hotel's own payroll, taxes, and insurance is different. The line between the two is in the loan documents and the guaranty.

**Transferring interests.** Admitting a new investor or selling a share of the borrower can count as a transfer of ownership, even when the aim is to raise money to cure the default. The usual route is to ask for the lender's consent in writing first.

**Filing for bankruptcy.** A filing can pause a foreclosure, which is why it tempts. Some guaranties treat a voluntary filing as a full-debt trigger.

## What happens to a guarantor in a negotiated ending

When a loan ends by agreement, in a [discounted payoff](/glossary/#discounted-payoff) or a [deed in lieu](/glossary/#deed-in-lieu), the owner usually asks for a release of the guaranty. The lender weighs what it gives up against what it receives. It gives up a claim against the guarantor, which is a possible source of recovery and of leverage. It receives the payment or the hotel without a contest, and saves time and cost. How that balance falls depends on the guarantor's finances and on whether a carve-out has been triggered.

A release is only as good as the signed agreement that grants it, and it may be partial, covering the debt while reserving claims for particular acts. A [note sale](/glossary/#note-sale) does not rewrite the guaranty. It passes to the new holder with the rest of the loan documents, as [what changes, and what does not, when your hotel loan is sold](/blog/when-your-hotel-loan-is-sold/) explains. What can change is the holder's willingness to agree to a release or a limit.

## What to check

- Read the guaranty itself, not the summary of terms.
- For each listed act, find out whether it makes the guarantor liable for losses or for the full debt.
- Read the definitions behind the triggers, such as transfer and bankruptcy.
- Note which triggers depend on a choice the borrower makes, and which can happen without one, such as an error by the manager.
- Before taking any step in a default that a carve-out might reach, such as a transfer, a filing, or moving cash between accounts, speak to the guarantor's own counsel. Where the lender can consent, ask in writing first.

## What we don't know

These documents vary by lender, by securitization, and by state. Whether a particular act triggers liability is a legal question about particular words. A post cannot say how a court would read an unclear clause, or whether a given holder would pursue a claim it has. Nothing here replaces counsel who has read the guaranty.
